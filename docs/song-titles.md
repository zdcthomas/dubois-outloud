# What these songs are called everywhere else

Fourteen of the eighteen songs have no approved recording. For most of them the
problem is the title. Du Bois used the names printed in the 1903 edition, and
the Library of Congress catalogues the same songs under other names. A search
that matches on his title will not find them.

This file lists the names to search instead. The variants come from the two
songbooks Du Bois drew on:

- William Francis Allen, Charles Pickard Ware and Lucy McKim Garrison,
  *Slave Songs of the United States* (1867) —
  <https://docsouth.unc.edu/church/allen/allen.html>
- Thomas P. Fenner's arrangements in M. F. Armstrong and Helen W. Ludlow,
  *Hampton and Its Students* (1874) —
  <https://docsouth.unc.edu/church/armstrong/armstrong.html>

Both are public domain and full-text online. Song numbers below refer to Allen.

## No candidates found at all

Nothing has ever been returned for these six, so there is nothing to review.

### Chapter 7 — Bright Sparkles in the Churchyard

- "Bright Sparkles in de Churchyard" (Fenner 1874)
- "Rock Me in the Cradle All the Day"

A composite. The cradle section is often catalogued as its own song, so the two
halves may sit under separate records.

### Chapter 10 — Steal Away

- "Steal Away to Jesus"

This is the diagnostic case. Steal Away is among the most recorded spirituals in
existence. Zero candidates means the search is failing, not that the Library has
nothing. Fix whatever drops this one and others will likely come back with it.

### Chapter 14 — Wrestlin' Jacob

- "Wrestle On, Jacob" (Allen 1867, no. 6)
- "Wrestling Jacob"

Two traps. Charles Wesley's hymn "Come, O Thou Traveler Unknown" is also known as
"Wrestling Jacob" and is a different piece. Allen's no. 83, "Wake Up, Jacob", is
also a different song.

### Chapter 14 — Do Bana Coba

- "Do bana coba, gene me, gene me"

Likely unfindable, and worth deciding early. Du Bois transcribed it from his
great-great-grandmother. It appears in no songbook and survives only through
*Souls*. Expect no field recording. Modern settings of the book exist, but those
are copyrighted performances rather than Library material. This song may have to
stay notation-only.

### Chapter 14 — Poor Rosy

- "Poor Rosy" (Allen 1867, no. 9)
- "Poor Rosy, Poor Gal"

A Sea Islands song. Look in the field collections, not the commercial discs.

### Chapter 14 — Weary Traveller

- "Don't Be Weary, Traveller" (Allen 1867, no. 98)

"Weary Traveler" also matches many unrelated gospel and country songs, so expect
noise.

## Why some rejections may have been right

Chapter 1 uses "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen", from Fenner 1874. Allen's
1867 book prints "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've **Had**" — and it carries
different music and different verses. Near-identical titles here can be genuinely
different songs. Some of the 143 rejects were probably right title, wrong tune.

## Where to look for different versions

### Library of Congress — already in the pipeline

Checked and live.

- **Southern Mosaic: the John and Ruby Lomax 1939 Southern States Recording
  Trip** — <https://www.loc.gov/collections/john-and-ruby-lomax/>
  Field recordings in churches and homes rather than studios. Two of the four
  approved recordings come from here.
- **National Jukebox** — <https://www.loc.gov/collections/national-jukebox/>
  Commercial discs, mostly pre-1925. This is where the Fisk Jubilee Singers and
  the Dinwiddie Colored Quartet sit.
- **Florida Folklife from the WPA Collections** —
  <https://www.loc.gov/collections/florida-folklife-from-the-works-progress-administration/>
  Not yet searched. Worth a pass for the Sea Islands repertoire.
- **Songs of America** — <https://www.loc.gov/collections/songs-of-america/>
  A cross-collection presentation with essays. Useful for finding the name a
  song travels under before searching the catalogue.

The approved recordings cluster in the Jukebox and the Lomax trip. The songs
with nothing found are mostly Sea Islands and Hampton repertoire, which sits in
different collections. A search tuned to one may not reach the other.

### Printed sources, for titles and tunes

- **Documenting the American South, "The Church in the Southern Black
  Community"** — <https://docsouth.unc.edu/church/>
  Holds both songbooks above, full text and page images.
- **Thomas Wentworth Higginson, *Army Life in a Black Regiment* (1870)** —
  Project Gutenberg eBook 6764. Describes several of these songs as sung in the
  field, including Wrestlin' Jacob and Lay This Body Down. Useful for variant
  first lines.
- **The 1903 first edition** —
  <https://archive.org/details/cu31924024920492>
  The source for the chapter 1 notation crop.

### Discography, to identify a version before searching

These two block automated requests but work in a browser. I could not verify
them from the command line.

- **Discography of American Historical Recordings (UC Santa Barbara)** —
  <https://adp.library.ucsb.edu/>
  The best tool for early commercial recordings. Search a song and it lists
  every take, label and matrix number, and often links straight to the National
  Jukebox copy. Use it to find which versions exist, then find them at the
  Library.
- **Smithsonian Folkways** — <https://folkways.si.edu/>
  Later field and revival recordings. Mostly still in copyright, so treat it as
  a finding aid rather than a source of audio for this site.

### The edition that produced our notation

- **The musical hypertext edition at way.net** —
  <https://way.net/SoulsOfBlackFolk/>
  Richard Cullen Rath set the book out online with the plates and commentary.
  Every chapter's notation except chapter 1 comes from here. Its audio links are
  all dead now except the MIDI files. The commentary cites the printed source
  for each song, which is where most of the variant titles above came from.
  The page carries injected pharmaceutical spam links; ignore them.
